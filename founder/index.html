The rider behind it

EJ

A donk rider first. An EV builder second. Florida-rooted, and building the car he wanted to exist.

The short version

EJ is from Florida. The cars were there before he was old enough to want one. His family and friends always had nice cars: donks, boxes, and bubbles, customized, decked out, and built with Florida style. Big wheels weren't a scene he discovered later — they were the driveway, the block, the parking lot after church.

His first car in the culture was a 1996 Chevy Impala SS — a bubble, in the culture's own words. That car introduced him to the world of big wheels: the paint, the audio, the shows, and the crews he's rolled with. Years of that came before the word "electric" ever entered it. Riding one first is why EJ never needed this culture explained to him — he lived it before he could name it.

Nobody had to sell EJ on these cars. What wore on him was everything the culture's been working around for thirty years — the center of mass that climbs with the lift, brakes asked to stop more than they were built for, suspension parts pulled from the truck aisle because nothing was ever made for this. None of that is a knock on the cars or the people who built around those limits for three decades. It's the reason somebody who actually rides needed to build the answer instead of waiting on a company that's never been to a show.

Electric wasn't the trend. It was the fix: instant torque, no heat soak sitting still, and a battery pack — which is to say weight he finally got to place exactly where the car needed it. A donk's whole engineering problem is where the weight sits once you lift it onto big wheels. A battery is just weight you get to choose the location of.

Florida is more than a location on this. Show season doesn't stop for weather, lots get packed and hot, and a gas engine idling in that kind of heat is doing its worst work exactly when a car is supposed to look its best. Building here means building for that — for humidity, for a state where these cars get driven and judged outdoors more months than not.

EDONKS started the way most of these things start: a car in the driveway, a drivetrain that didn't match the vision, and a rider who wouldn't accept that those were the only two options.

Why electric, from a rider

Nobody in this scene needs to be sold on effort. Riders already spend years and real money getting a car to sit right and finish clean. Electric isn't a shortcut around that work — it's a different answer to the same question: how do you make this car do what you see in your head?

Every rider in this scene has felt it: real money chasing a stance and a shine, then the afternoon lost to a temperature gauge in a packed lot. That's not a culture problem. That's a drivetrain problem — and a drivetrain can be replaced without touching anything that makes the car what it is.

The honest version: heat, noise you didn't ask for, and a drivetrain that fights you in show traffic are solvable problems now. So solve them, and keep everything else.
