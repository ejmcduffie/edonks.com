What it is

The E-Box is a modular electric chassis platform born out of the E1. The name nods to the box Chevy — the cars that carried this culture — and the job is simple to say and hard to do: give big-wheel builds a foundation that was engineered for them instead of adapted to them.

For thirty years, riders have been solving real engineering problems with parts sourced from somewhere else. Lift a car and run 26s and the center of mass climbs with it. Stock brakes get asked to stop a rotating package they were never sized for. Suspension arms and spindles come out of the truck aisle because that's what's strong enough to exist. None of that is a knock on the builders — it's what happens when a culture outgrows the parts catalog. The E-Box is the catalog catching up.

A battery is heavy, and heavy is only a problem when you can't choose where it goes. The E-Box design puts the pack low and centered, pulling the center of mass back down while the body keeps the stance riders build for. Brakes are specified against the actual wheel and tire package. Suspension geometry is designed for the ride height, not corrected toward it. And because it's modular, one platform is designed to sit under a donk, a box, or a bubble. All of that is the design today; the E1 is where it gets proven.

Eight subsystems, one system

The modules

Eight subsystems, each specified on paper against the wheel package, not against a stock 1974 parts book. Specs are written; hardware comes with the E1.

  1. 01

    Frame

    The spine. Rails sized for pack integration and body mounting, with ride height for big wheels designed in rather than lifted in afterward.

  2. 02

    Front suspension & steering

    Purpose-built geometry for the ride height riders actually run — instead of truck spindles and arms adapted into the job.

  3. 03

    Rear drive

    Motor, reduction, and rear suspension packaged together, placed for balance and for the stance to sit correct.

  4. 04

    Battery

    Modular pack between the rails and under the floor. The heaviest thing in the car, put where it lowers the center of mass instead of raising it.

  5. 05

    Brakes

    Sized to rolling diameter, front and rear, with regen handling the routine slowing so the friction brakes stay fresh.

  6. 06

    Power electronics & charging

    Inverter, DC-DC, onboard charger, HV distribution, and a charge port that doesn't fight the body lines.

  7. 07

    Body-mount interface

    The defined handshake between chassis and body. Mount points, floor clearance, and firewall pass-throughs documented so a shop can plan a build before it starts.

  8. 08

    Cooling, HVAC & 12V

    Thermal loops for pack and drive, cabin heat and A/C with no engine to borrow from, and the low-voltage system that runs your lights, locks, and system.

Module specs are still in engineering, not marketing. We'd rather ship this page with no numbers than a number we can't stand behind.

Under the hood, honestly

Fit, assumptions, safety

The short version of the engineering file. What it's designed to fit, what it's betting on, and how it's kept safe.

What it's designed to fit

  • —'71–'76 Impala and Caprice first — the E1 and the first rider cars.
  • —'77–'90 box and '91–'96 bubble next: same frame family, different clip lengths and body mounts.
  • —G-body and Ford Panther (Crown Vic, Grand Marquis, Town Car) later, each with its own clips and body-mount adapters.
  • —Wheelbases from 108" to 125" through swappable front and rear clips on one common center section.
  • —24" through 30" wheels, sized at 26" and checked at 30". Rake stays — nose up, the way it should sit.

What it's betting on

  • —The lift makes room for the pack. On a lifted car on big wheels, there's new space under the floor where a flat battery can live.
  • —The frame has to be wide enough between the rails for the pack. That gets measured on the E1 with a tape before anything is cut.
  • —The car gets heavier than a gas donk. The bet is that weight sitting low makes it more stable, not less. That gets proven on scales and a tilt test, not on paper.
  • —Every wheel and tire setup has to be load-rated for the car's weight. If a fitment can't be, it's off the menu, and we'll say so.

How it's kept safe

  • —Friction brakes sized to stop the car with regen off and the high-voltage system down. Regen helps; it never stands in for brakes.
  • —Isolation monitoring always on, a manual service disconnect, and an interlock that opens the main contactors in hardware.
  • —No high-voltage wiring through the cabin. Crash sensor opens the contactors; a pyrofuse protects the pack.
  • —Liquid-cooled pack, designed for a Florida lot at 95°F with the car sitting still.
  • —Built to modern EV safety standards (FMVSS 305, SAE J1766 intent) voluntarily. A conversion shop can't crash-test, so the engineering is documented so it can be checked.
